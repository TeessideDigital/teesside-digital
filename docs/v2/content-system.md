## 1. Brand voice

### Core voice

**APPROVED CONTENT PRINCIPLE:** Write as a capable professional explaining useful work to an intelligent business owner. Be clear about what can be done, what the decision depends on and what is known. Confidence comes from specifics and responsibility, not superlatives.

| Characteristic | How it appears in writing |
|---|---|
| Clear | Name the thing, the action and the purpose. Prefer "booking form" to "customer engagement solution". |
| Competent | Explain a relevant decision or constraint. Avoid lists of technologies as substitutes for understanding. |
| Precise | Distinguish a proposed improvement from an implemented feature and a measured result. |
| Approachable | Use ordinary words, direct address and natural contractions where useful. |
| Grounded | Acknowledge existing systems, available time and manageable scope. |
| Human | Identify who does the work. Avoid an anonymous corporate voice or exaggerated intimacy. |
| Commercially useful | Help the reader recognise fit, understand an option or take a sensible next step. |

### Sentence and vocabulary style

Use active verbs, mostly short-to-medium sentences and one main idea per paragraph. Aim for roughly 12-22 words in routine sentences and 2-4 sentences in explanatory paragraphs, but treat these as editing guides rather than rigid limits. A qualification that prevents misunderstanding is worth keeping. Avoid chains of clipped slogan fragments and repeated rhetorical questions.

Use British English and ASCII punctuation throughout. Use sentence case for ordinary headings, controls and status labels; retain the approved service names and formal degree titles. Use ampersands in those service names, not as a default substitute for "and" in prose. Prefer "enquiry", "organisation", "programme" where appropriate, and "licence" as the noun. Do not write theatrical ellipses, excessive exclamation marks or strings of slashes to imitate a studio identity.

Technical language has three levels: a recognisable business situation, the behaviour of a possible solution, and optional implementation detail. Introduce each level only when it adds meaning. Plain language must preserve the real limitation, including where a person still needs to check a result.

### First person and company voice

- Use **Teesside Digital** for the offer, location and business-level positioning.
- Use **I** for Dave's judgement, communication and personal actions, with his identity established nearby or in context.
- Use **you/your business** to address the reader without assuming their business is failing.
- Use **we** only for a clearly shared action between Dave and the customer, or another genuine collaboration. Make that referent explicit.
- Avoid "our team", "our specialists", "contact us" and "we deliver" as default agency language. Do not alternate singular and plural identities for variety.

**CONDITIONAL EXAMPLE of a shared action:** "You and I agree the scope before development begins." Use only when this is the adopted delivery process. It is clearer than an unexplained "we".

Humour should be incidental and light, never required. Do not joke in errors, privacy explanations, accessibility instructions or descriptions of a customer's difficulty. No forced local slang, self-deprecation about studying or jokes about agencies charging too much.

Sound premium through clarity of scope, thoughtful explanation and reliable communication. Do not repeatedly call the work premium, exclusive, world-class or affordable. Neither modest language nor solo delivery requires bargain positioning.

### Preferred and avoided language

| Prefer | Avoid | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Build, improve, connect, review, test, explain | Elevate, unlock, revolutionise, supercharge | Name the action rather than an undefined transformation. |
| Website, booking tool, enquiry form, quoting workflow | Digital solution, ecosystem, experience platform | Identify what the customer would actually use. |
| Project, scope, agreed work, next step | Journey, discovery sprint, engagement framework everywhere | Do not require consultancy vocabulary to understand ordinary work. |
| Direct contact with Dave | No middlemen, no juniors, no agency nonsense | Describe the operating model without attacking others. |
| Designed to reduce repeated entry | Saves hours every week | An intended benefit is not a measured result. |
| Security-conscious development with defined checks | Secure by default, bulletproof, no vulnerabilities | Avoid blanket assurance unsupported by scope and evidence. |

### Good and bad phrasing

The better examples below demonstrate tone; technical or operating claims still require the relevant checks.

| Avoid | Better direction |
|---|---|
| "Unlock your business's full digital potential." | "A clearer website can help customers understand what you offer and how to enquire." |
| "Our experts build future-ready systems." | "Teesside Digital builds websites, business tools and practical automation. You'll work directly with Dave." |
| "Stop wasting time on outdated processes." | "If the same information is being entered twice, it may be worth simplifying the process." |
| "AI will transform your operations." | "Some repeated tasks suit ordinary rules. Others may benefit from AI, with checks on the output." |
| "I am a student looking to gain experience." | "I run Teesside Digital and work directly with clients. I'm also studying at The Open University." |
| "Bespoke craftsmanship, forged in Teesside." | "Based in Hartlepool, working with small businesses across Teesside." |

## 4. Problem language system

Use these as a vocabulary for discovery and service explanation. They are possible situations, not diagnoses of the visitor. Begin with conditional language when describing a problem that has not been established.

### Understanding the business and making contact

| PROBLEM: what the owner experiences | POSSIBLE RESPONSE | TECHNICAL LAYER: only where useful |
|---|---|---|
| Customers keep asking what the business actually provides. | Clarify the offer, examples and enquiry instructions. A content edit may be enough. | Content structure and semantic page markup when explaining implementation. |
| People cannot find the next step on a phone. | Improve navigation, enquiry placement and form usability. | Responsive layout, keyboard access and form behaviour. |
| An enquiry appears sent but never reaches the intended recipient. | Trace the current delivery path and fix acknowledgement or routing. | Form receiver, delivery records and failure handling. Do not assume the cause from appearance alone. |
| The website is difficult to update accurately. | Simplify the editing process, remove duplicated content or choose a suitable existing publishing tool. | Content model or content management system, introduced after the editing need is understood. |

### Running everyday tasks

| PROBLEM: what the owner experiences | POSSIBLE RESPONSE | TECHNICAL LAYER: only where useful |
|---|---|---|
| Quotes require repeatedly copying prices and customer details. | Improve the template or spreadsheet first; build a quoting tool if rules and volume justify it. | Input validation, calculation rules and document output. AI is not required for a known calculation. |
| Booking requires repeated messages to establish availability. | Configure an appropriate booking service or develop a bounded booking workflow where a clear gap remains. | Scheduling integration, availability rules and confirmations. |
| Staff use different versions of the same record. | Agree ownership of the record and improve shared access before adding software. | A shared application, permissions or database only where justified. |
| Customers repeatedly ask for an update on an existing request. | Improve communication or add a simple status view if the information can be maintained reliably. | A customer portal and access controls where needed, not a portal by default. |

### Moving information between systems

| PROBLEM: what the owner experiences | POSSIBLE RESPONSE | TECHNICAL LAYER: only where useful |
|---|---|---|
| The same details are typed into two systems. | Remove unnecessary duplication, use an existing integration or connect the systems if supported. | API connection, data mapping and validation. Explain an API as a supported way for systems to exchange information. |
| Enquiries sit unassigned in an inbox. | Agree who owns the task; add routing or reminders if the process is clear. | Rules-based automation, failure alerts and a recoverable record of actions. |
| A process stops silently when a third-party service is unavailable. | Make failures visible and define what someone should do next. | Error handling, retry rules and logs. Do not promise a system can never fail. |

### Working with documents and repetitive text

| PROBLEM: what the owner experiences | POSSIBLE RESPONSE | TECHNICAL LAYER: only where useful |
|---|---|---|
| Information is manually copied from consistent forms. | Improve the input format or use rule-based extraction before considering AI. | Structured data, parsing and validation. |
| Varied documents take time to sort or summarise. | Test an AI-assisted draft or classification step with a person checking the result, if the data and risk are suitable. | Model selection, evaluation against examples, data handling and human review. |
| Standard responses are written repeatedly. | Improve reusable templates; consider assisted drafting only if variation makes it useful. | Templates first; a language model may help draft text but does not establish factual accuracy. |

Avoid building the copy around fear or incompetence. A manual process may be sensible at low volume. A better responsibility rule, clearer wording or an existing product may solve the problem more economically than custom work. No example above establishes that Teesside Digital has already delivered that solution for a customer.

## 5. Service messaging

These are approved service descriptions and scope examples. They preserve the approved catalogue without converting every possible capability into a claim of specialist experience. Actual engagements remain subject to fit, competence and manageable delivery scope.

### A. Websites & Web Development

**Approved plain-language description:**

> Websites that explain what your business offers and give customers a clear way to take the next step. This can mean improving an existing site or designing and building a new one.

**Problems addressed:** Unclear service information, awkward mobile use, weak enquiry routes, difficult content updates and a website that does not support the business's actual needs.

**Appropriate work:** Business websites, landing pages for a defined offer, content and enquiry improvements, responsive frontends, suitable ecommerce work and integrations with existing services. Consider an established commerce or publishing platform where it fits. Do not imply that every site must be hand-built from nothing.

**Inappropriate or over-scoped work:** Rebuilding a payment platform or full commerce infrastructure merely to demonstrate skill; taking on large mission-critical platforms beyond solo capacity; treating marketing strategy, ongoing search campaigns or guaranteed lead generation as automatically included in web development.

**Outcomes that can be discussed:** A clearer explanation of the offer, a more usable route to enquiry and content that is easier to maintain. Before delivery, these are aims. After delivery, describe specific changes that can be inspected. More enquiries, higher conversion and revenue growth require actual evidence and cannot be inferred from a new design.

**Technical capabilities that may be mentioned:** Astro, TypeScript, semantic HTML, responsive CSS, appropriate React use, forms, integrations, structured content and SEO-aware implementation. Name the stack beside relevant work where it explains a decision. Do not use the existing stack as evidence of broad expertise with every platform.

**Embedded standards:** Address input handling and third-party exposure; usable navigation, labels and keyboard access; image/font delivery and avoidable script cost. State what has actually been checked on a particular site. Security-conscious development is not a guarantee of security.

**Likely CTA:** "Discuss your website" as a contextual route to the primary enquiry. "Request a website review" remains the separate secondary action for an existing site.

**Avoid:** "High-converting websites", "guaranteed rankings", "pixel-perfect on every device", "fully accessible", "no templates ever", and claims that an attractive new site will automatically generate customers.

### B. Apps & Digital Tools

**Approved plain-language description:**

> Practical tools for tasks such as quoting, booking and managing information. The starting point is what people need to do and whether an existing system can already support it.

**Problems addressed:** Repeated calculations, awkward record management, fragmented task handling, avoidable copying and workflows that no longer fit a basic document or spreadsheet.

**Appropriate work:** A bounded quoting tool, an internal task application, a customer portal, a useful dashboard tied to real decisions, or a booking workflow with a clear requirement. A web application or PWA may be suitable; a native mobile application needs a genuine reason and a supportable scope.

**Inappropriate or over-scoped work:** A speculative all-in-one business platform, enterprise resource planning replacement, safety-critical system, complex regulated application without suitable expertise, or a native app commissioned solely for the presence of an app-store listing.

**Outcomes that can be discussed:** Completing a defined task in one place, applying agreed calculation rules consistently and making the right information available to appropriate users. Quantified time savings, error reduction and staff adoption remain evidence-dependent. A dashboard is not useful merely because it displays charts.

**Technical capabilities that may be mentioned:** Web applications, interfaces, validation, authentication, permissions, data modelling, integrations and testing of agreed behaviour where Dave can support the scope. Explain authentication as establishing who is using a system and permissions as what they are allowed to do when that distinction matters. Do not imply that the present marketing repository demonstrates a production customer portal.

**Embedded standards:** Appropriate access and input controls; usable forms, error recovery and keyboard workflows; responsiveness with representative data and realistic task sizes. Agree how data, failures and maintenance will be handled rather than promising permanent reliability.

**Likely CTA:** "Discuss a business tool". Use the same problem-led enquiry approach; do not make prospects specify their preferred architecture.

**Avoid:** "Any app you can imagine", "enterprise-grade", "infinitely scalable", "build your next unicorn", and references to QuoteFlow as a finished application.

### C. Automation & AI

**Approved plain-language description:**

> Automation for repetitive work and information that needs to move between systems. AI can assist where it is useful, with clear limits and appropriate checks on the results.

**Problems addressed:** Duplicate data entry, repetitive administration, predictable routing, document handling and drafting work where a person currently repeats a similar step.

**Appropriate work:** Defined enquiry routing, supported system integrations, reminders, document generation from known data, and carefully evaluated AI-assisted extraction, classification or drafting. Start with the process and data quality. Use ordinary rules where the task has a known answer.

**Inappropriate or over-scoped work:** Promising autonomous operation of a whole business; unreviewed high-stakes decisions; indiscriminate processing of sensitive information; workforce-replacement guarantees; integrations without supported access; complex predictive modelling without adequate data, expertise and validation.

**Outcomes that can be discussed:** Less repeated handling as an aim, a clearer handoff and a defined person responsible when a step fails. An implemented workflow can demonstrate that an action is routed or a document produced. It cannot establish real savings, accuracy or commercial benefit without measurement in use.

**Technical capabilities that may be mentioned:** Rules-based workflows, APIs, scheduled tasks, webhooks and carefully scoped language-model use where competence supports delivery. Explain a webhook as one system notifying another when an event happens. Only introduce model terminology where it clarifies behaviour or limits.

**Embedded standards:** Approved data access and handling, appropriate permissions, visible failure paths, human review where needed, accessible interfaces for people using the workflow, and consideration of response time, cost and third-party limits. AI-generated output can be wrong; the copy must say what is checked and by whom for the specific use case.

**Likely CTA:** "Discuss a repetitive task". The label encourages a recognisable starting point without promising that automation is the right answer.

**Avoid:** "AI-powered everything", "set and forget", "eliminate all admin", "never makes mistakes", "replace your team", "guaranteed ROI" and "saves hours every week" without a supporting measurement.

### How the services relate

**Approved connecting explanation:**

> A website helps people understand the business and make contact. A tool supports a task people need to complete. Automation handles defined repeated steps or moves information between systems. They can work together, but you may only need one.

Some projects span categories. Classify by the customer's main task rather than duplicating the same claim across three service descriptions. Security is not an equal fourth service pillar. Bounded security review or hardening may be discussed only where the proposed work and competence support it; do not turn the manual website review into an implied security engagement.

## 6. Security, accessibility and performance language

### Communication rules

| Standard | Safe direction for describing intended practice | Requires specific evidence | Must not be claimed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Security | "Security considerations form part of the design and development work." Use once this is an adopted practice. | Named checks, identified issues, fixes, test scope and current dependency/configuration findings. | "No vulnerabilities", guaranteed security, unhackable, certification implied by study, or broad specialist services outside approved scope. |
| Accessibility | "WCAG 2.2 AA is the accessibility target for the work." | Which pages and tasks were tested, methods, environments, findings and unresolved limitations. | "Fully accessible", works for everyone, complete conformance based on automated scores, or assistive-technology testing that did not happen. |
| Performance | "Page weight and responsiveness are considered during development." Use once adopted. | Actual loading or interaction measurements, date, device/environment, method and limits. | Lightning-fast everywhere, unsupported Lighthouse scores, guaranteed speed for every visitor or a target presented as an achieved result. |

An allowed practice statement still commits Dave to doing the work. It is not a way to evade evidence by replacing "secure" with "security-conscious". Avoid a trio of badges standing in for an explanation. Tie standards to the task: what was done, why it mattered and what remains unknown.

### Evidence beside work

**CONDITIONAL EXAMPLES - publish only after the described behaviour is implemented and checked:**

- "This form validates submitted information at the receiving boundary as well as in the browser. Browser feedback helps the person filling it in; it is not the only check."
- "The error message identifies the field that needs attention and explains how to correct it. Keyboard testing covered this submission path."
- "The page sends a smaller image to a narrow screen. The comparison below records the files and test conditions used."

These examples do not claim that V1 currently behaves this way. For measured evidence, use a record with **subject, date, environment, method, result and limitations**. Fill it with verified facts later; do not create plausible-looking example numbers for publication.

Keep project performance targets separate from results: LCP <2.5s, CLS <0.1, INP <200ms and Lighthouse targets of 90/95/95/95 are existing goals, not current achievement claims. A passing build or automated score cannot establish the full quality of an experience.

### Controlled terminology

| Term | When it may be used | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Demonstration | Work made to explore or show capability, clearly distinguished from commissioned client work. | Real customers, adoption or commercial results. |
| Work in progress | A project with meaningful work underway and material work still remaining. State the relevant limits. | That the current version is complete, reliable in every path or ready for production use. |
| Early development | Work has started but useful functionality is still limited. Normally internal context at the present proof threshold. | A portfolio-ready application. |
| Implemented | A named feature exists in the identified version and can be inspected. | It has been comprehensively tested, deployed or used successfully by customers. |
| Measured | A value was recorded using an identified method, date, environment and scope. | That it applies to every user, that the change caused a business result or that a target was achieved universally. |
| Tested | A named check was actually performed under stated conditions, with an outcome recorded. | Complete coverage or a pass unless the outcome is stated. |
| Observed | A specific behaviour or condition was seen in a defined context. | A quantified measurement, universal behaviour or an explanation of its cause. |
| Target | A desired threshold or outcome to work towards. | An achieved result. Keep targets separate from results visually and verbally. |
| Provisional | A decision or detail remains under evaluation and may change. | A production commitment. Usually internal documentation language. |
| Client project | Genuine work commissioned by an identifiable client, with appropriate permission for public presentation. | Permission to disclose confidential information, endorse the business or invent a testimonial. |
| Internal project | Work for Teesside Digital's own use or development. | External client experience. A demonstration can also be internal; state the relationship clearly. |
| Completed | The stated scope is finished, with relevant acceptance or completion evidence. | No future maintenance, no defects or completion of every imagined feature. It must not describe unfinished scope. |

Project type, delivery status, deployment state and evidence are separate facts. A live demonstration can still be work in progress. A completed internal project is still not client work. Avoid a single badge that collapses those distinctions.

### Evidence-writing pattern

For each meaningful claim, write:

1. What the reader is looking at or what was done.
2. Why that decision was relevant to the task.
3. What evidence supports the statement.
4. What the evidence does not establish or what remains unfinished, when material.

**CONDITIONAL EXAMPLE:** "The quoting form applies the agreed pricing rules to the entered quantities. The calculation can be inspected in the demonstration. This shows the calculation behaviour; it does not show time saved in a customer's business." Use only for a working, verified feature, not as current QuoteFlow copy.

Use captions to explain an actual screen or behaviour. Useful labels include "Project type", "Current status", "What was implemented", "Test method" and "Limitations" where needed. Avoid fake document numbers, decorative revision stamps and long technology tags as evidence substitutes. Status and limitations must stay attached to excerpts as they are reused elsewhere.

## 9. Claims framework

### Four levels of evidence

The levels describe the kind of substantiation needed, not a ranking of sales value. A simple factual claim can be more useful than a weak metric. A higher-level claim does not automatically validate lower-level claims about how the result was produced.

| Level | Type | Evidence required | Example and boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| LEVEL 1 | Factual operating claim | Dave-confirmed current fact or a real, adopted operating arrangement. Recheck if circumstances change. | "Teesside Digital is based in Hartlepool." Direct-delivery statements must match who actually does the work. |
| LEVEL 2 | Demonstrated technical claim | Inspectable implementation/version, a defined behaviour and checks sufficient to support the statement. Record what is not covered. | Conditional: "The form identifies an invalid email address before submission." This does not establish delivery, full accessibility or server-side validation. |
| LEVEL 3 | Measured claim | Raw result, subject/version, method, date, environment, scope, comparison baseline where relevant and limitations. | A page-loading measurement reported with its test conditions. No illustrative numeric result is supplied here. |
| LEVEL 4 | Customer/commercial outcome claim | Genuine client/use context, permission where needed, baseline and follow-up evidence, measurement period and a justified account of attribution or uncertainty. | A documented reduction in time spent on a task in actual use. Do not claim the software alone caused the change if staffing or process also changed. |

Targets and design intentions sit outside the achieved-claim ladder. Label them as aims. "Designed to reduce repeated entry" is not equivalent to "reduced repeated entry", and still needs an actual design rationale rather than wishful wording.

### Publication check

## 18. Content accessibility

These are writing requirements supporting the WCAG 2.2 AA target. They are not a claim of conformance or a replacement for interface testing.

- **Headings:** Name the subject and support scanning. A reader reviewing headings alone should understand the main topics. Avoid clever labels that conceal services, work or contact.
- **Links:** Make purpose clear in context and distinguish destinations. Do not reuse the same vague label for unrelated work. Avoid "click here", position-only instructions and links whose text promises a different action.
- **Plain language:** Prefer a familiar word where it preserves meaning. Explain the practical consequence of a technical choice. Do not replace an important limitation with a cheerful simplification.
- **Forms:** State required information and useful format guidance before the error occurs. Keep hints outside placeholder-only text. Use concrete labels and identify optional information.
- **Errors:** Explain what needs changing and the recovery action. Avoid blame and technical codes as the only message. Distinguish rejected submission from unknown delivery state.
- **Acronyms:** Expand at first substantive use when needed. "Artificial intelligence (AI)" can introduce AI in explanatory prose; the approved service title remains Automation & AI. Do not force every technical acronym into public copy.
- **Technical terms:** Give the working meaning, not a dictionary digression. "An integration connects two systems so information can move between them" is useful where a customer needs to understand that scope.
- **Length:** Use the sentence and paragraph guidance in section 1. Split a long explanation at a logical boundary, not into a dozen slogan fragments. Use lists for genuine parallel items.
- **Cognitive load:** Put the main point first, keep related facts together and offer detail only where useful. Preserve project status and material limitations even in short excerpts.
- **Evidence:** Describe the purpose of meaningful visuals in text. Alt text identifies relevant visual information; a caption can explain the decision. Avoid repeating the same sentence in both without a reason.
- **Consistency:** Use the same label for the same service, action and status across contexts. Responsive adaptation must not change the meaning or hide a limitation.

Do not rely on colour, motion, pointer location or a screenshot to convey essential information. The side index's unusual placement increases the value of familiar wording. More technical detail is not automatically more accessible or more credible.
